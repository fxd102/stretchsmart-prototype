# Family Caregiver Discovery Interview Guide

Five conversations. Adult children currently managing a parent's care. The
goal is not to validate a product — the goal is to **learn the real shape**
of what families need, in their words, before you commit any contractor
money.

Faith's own brief calls this prerequisite non-negotiable. It's right. Five
conversations cost you ten hours and save you a year of misbuild.

---

## Ground rules (the Mom Test, condensed)

1. **Talk about THEIR life, not your idea.** The phrase "would you use an
   app that..." is forbidden in this conversation. Replace with
   "tell me what you did last [week / time this came up]."
2. **Past behavior, not future intent.** "Have you ever paid for help
   with this?" beats "Would you pay for help with this?" every time.
3. **Ask for specifics, not abstractions.** "What happened Tuesday?"
   not "How does communication usually go?"
4. **Let silence work.** If they pause, don't fill the space. The most
   important thing they'll tell you comes after the second silence.
5. **Listen for what they bring up unprompted.** The unprompted topic is
   ten times the signal of an answer to your direct question.
6. **No pitching until the very end, if at all.** Don't describe what you
   might build. If they ask, deflect: "I'm still trying to understand the
   problem before I figure out what to make."

---

## Pre-interview

**Recruit through warm channels first.** Friends, friends-of-friends,
community contacts. You only need 5.

**Send a short note in advance:**

> Hi [name] — Faith here. I'd love to set up a 60-minute call to learn
> about how you've been managing your [mom/dad]'s care. I'm doing some
> research into how families coordinate this kind of thing and your
> experience would be really valuable. No product to pitch — I'm just
> trying to understand the real shape of it. Would [day / day] work?
> Either Zoom or phone, your call.

**Have ready:**
- A notebook or open doc for notes (don't rely on memory).
- Ask about recording on the call, never in advance. Saying "would you
  mind if I record this so I can listen back?" usually gets a yes.
- Be in a place you won't be interrupted.

---

## Opening (5 minutes) — warm up, set context

Goals: rapport, low stakes, set tone, get permission.

> Thanks so much for doing this. To set the scene — I'm doing research
> into how adult children coordinate care for an aging parent. There's
> no product I'm pitching; I'm trying to understand what the real
> day-to-day actually looks like. About sixty minutes, you can skip
> anything that feels too personal, and I'll be taking notes. Mind if I
> record? It just helps me listen better in the moment.
>
> Before we go deep — how are you doing today? Just generally.

Let them answer. Don't rush.

---

## Module 1 — Their situation (10 minutes)

Goal: understand the elder, the family configuration, and how Faith's
interviewee ended up in the caregiver role.

- Tell me about your [mom/dad]. Where are they living, what kind of care
  do they need right now?
- How did you come to be the person managing this?
- Who else in the family is involved? Are you all in the same place,
  different cities?
- How long has this been the situation? Has the level of care changed
  in the last year?

**Listen for:** how they describe their role ("I'm the one who..."),
geographic spread, sibling dynamics, whether the elder is still in their
own home / assisted living / memory care / with the interviewee.

---

## Module 2 — A specific recent moment (15 minutes) — the gold

This is the most important section. Get them to walk you through real
time, not aggregated experience.

> Can you walk me through the last week of dealing with [mom/dad]'s
> care? Just literally what happened — Monday, Tuesday. Doesn't have to
> be dramatic. I want to understand what the rhythm actually looks
> like.

If they generalize ("usually I..."), gently re-anchor: "Was that last
week specifically, or in general? Tell me about last week."

**Probe:**
- What was the hardest moment?
- What was something that should have been easier but wasn't?
- Did anything come up that you weren't expecting?
- Did you have to explain something to a sibling, a doctor, or a
  facility staff member more than once?

**Listen for:** specific friction points, things they had to chase
down, conversations they had to repeat, paperwork they couldn't find,
times they were caught off guard.

---

## Module 3 — Information & memory (10 minutes)

Goal: surface the information-management problem that the platform is
hypothetically solving.

- How do you keep track of your [mom/dad]'s medications right now?
- Where do you keep their advance directive / POA / healthcare proxy?
- When something changes — a new doctor, a new medication, a new
  diagnosis — how does that information get to everyone in the family
  who needs to know?
- Has there been a time recently when someone in the family (or a
  provider) didn't have information they should have had? What
  happened?

**Listen for:** spreadsheets, group texts, repeated explanations,
forgotten facts, anxiety about gaps. Note WHERE the information lives
(phones, paper, memory, an app). Note who is "the keeper."

---

## Module 4 — Communication & coordination (10 minutes)

Goal: surface the sibling / family dynamic.

- Tell me about the last time you and a sibling had a misunderstanding
  about [mom/dad]'s care. What happened?
- How do you and the family make decisions together — about facilities,
  procedures, the bigger choices?
- What do you wish your siblings did differently in this whole thing?
  What do you think they wish you did differently?
- Is there anything you avoid talking about?

**Listen for:** unresolved tension, who's seen as doing too much / too
little, decision-making patterns, financial conversations, things going
unsaid.

---

## Module 5 — What they've tried (5 minutes)

Goal: understand the substitute set.

- Have you ever used any apps, tools, or services to help with the
  coordination side of this?
- What was good / not good about them?
- Are you in any online groups or communities of people dealing with
  similar things?
- Has anyone — a friend, a coworker, a social worker — recommended
  anything useful to you?

**Listen for:** specific products by name, what they tried and
abandoned, what they kept. Substitutes are competition.

---

## Module 6 — The wallet question (5 minutes, careful)

Do NOT ask "would you pay for X?" That answer is meaningless.

Ask instead:

- What have you actually spent money on related to [mom/dad]'s care
  that wasn't medical? Anything at all — books, apps, services, an
  organizer, a consultant, anything?
- If you've spent money — what was the moment that pushed you to spend
  it? What was the breaking point?
- Has anything been so frustrating that you've thought "I'd pay
  somebody to just handle this"?

**Listen for:** past spending behavior, the moments that pushed them
over the edge, the specific phrases they use about what they'd pay
for. This is your pricing and positioning research, more than any
survey could give you.

---

## Module 7 — The wand (5 minutes) — let them tell you the product

> If you had a magic wand — not for the medical part, but for the
> coordination, communication, information part — what would it do?

Then **stop talking.** Let them think. Whatever they say first is
worth a hundred features you'd come up with.

If they ask "like what?" resist the urge to give an example. Say:
"anything — whatever you imagine would make this easier."

---

## Closing (5 minutes)

> This was incredibly helpful. Thank you.
>
> One last thing — I'm doing four more of these conversations. If I
> end up building something based on what I learn, would it be okay if
> I came back to you in three to six months and showed you what I made?
> No commitment on your end, I'd just love to know if I got it right.

Plants the warm-lead seed. Most people will say yes.

---

## After the call

Do the debrief **immediately** (within an hour). Memory decays fast.

## Reminders to yourself, Faith

- Their answer to "what would make this easier?" is more valuable than
  their answer to "would you pay $15/mo for an app?" — by 100×.
- The truth is in what they DID, not in what they say they'd do.
- If three of five interviewees bring up the SAME unprompted thing,
  that's a feature. Build it.
- If you find yourself defending an idea during the conversation, stop.
  You're pitching. Reset.
- Your authenticity ("I'm a high myope" worked for HighMyopes; here
  it's "I'm someone thinking about building a boutique elder care
  facility and trying to understand families I'd want to serve") is
  your unfair advantage. Use it.
- If a conversation reveals the family-coordination premise is wrong,
  THAT IS THE WIN. You just saved $50k.
