# Restructuring a Profitable Business Into Charitable Service
### A one-pager to bring to your CPA and nonprofit attorney

*Prepared for a financially-secure owner (debt-free, retirement-set) who is
comfortable giving up the business assets and wants only a modest ongoing
salary, with most income going to a real charity (scholarships, a girls'
home, or similar).*

This profile is unusually clean. The hardest friction in these deals —
a founder who secretly wants to keep the wealth — does not apply to you.
That removes the main legal danger zone (private-benefit / inurement) and
makes the structures below straightforward. Your job is mostly to pick the
right vehicle and document things correctly.

---

## The core fact

A 501(c)(3) has **no owners and no equity**. Its assets are permanently
locked to the charitable purpose. You give up the business value — which
you have said you are fine with — and in exchange the income it generates
serves the charity without flowing to you as taxable personal wealth. You
draw a salary for the work you actually do, nothing more.

---

## Recommended structure for your situation: the "Newman's Own" model

Because you want a commercial business to keep running while its profits go
to charity, the strongest fit is a **foundation that wholly owns the
operating company**:

1. **Form a 501(c)(3)** (likely a *private foundation*, since one business
   funds it — your attorney will confirm vs. public charity).
2. **Donate your ownership interest in the LLC to the foundation.** The
   foundation now owns the business. You receive a charitable deduction for
   the gift (see the valuation note below — this is the one number to get
   right).
3. **The company keeps operating commercially.** Its profits flow up to the
   foundation, its owner, which deploys them to the mission.
4. **You become a salaried employee/manager** of the company or foundation,
   drawing modest, defensible "reasonable compensation."

**The key legal hook to name to your attorney:** Internal Revenue Code
**Section 4943(g), the "Newman's Own" exception** (enacted 2018). Normally a
private foundation cannot own more than 20% of a business (the
"excess business holdings" rule). Section 4943(g) carves out an exception
allowing a foundation to **wholly own** a business if 100% of the profits go
to charity and independence requirements are met. This exception is exactly
what makes your plan possible — make sure your attorney is building around
it.

### Alternative: convert the operations directly into the nonprofit
If the business *activity itself* is or can be charitable, the operating
entity can simply become the 501(c)(3). Income tied to the exempt purpose is
untaxed; unrelated commercial income is taxed (see UBIT below). This is
cleaner *if* the work maps to a charitable purpose, and messier if the
business is purely commercial — in which case the Newman's Own model above
is better.

---

## The three things you must get right

1. **Valuation of the donated business (your deduction).** A gift of a
   closely-held business interest over a small threshold requires a
   *qualified appraisal*. **Critical planning point:** gifts of
   non-publicly-traded assets to a *private foundation* are often deductible
   only at your **cost basis**, not fair market value, while gifts to a
   *public charity* may get full fair-market-value treatment. This single
   distinction can swing your deduction enormously — have your CPA model
   both before you choose the vehicle.

2. **Reasonable compensation (your salary).** It must be benchmarked to
   market rate for the actual work you do, board-approved, and documented.
   Overpaying yourself is an "excess benefit transaction" with real IRS
   penalties. Since you only want minimal living-expense income, this is
   easy for you to satisfy — just document it.

3. **Unrelated Business Income Tax (UBIT).** Commercial profit that is not
   related to the charitable purpose is taxed even inside/under a nonprofit.
   Often handled by holding the commercial activity in a taxable subsidiary
   owned by the foundation. Your attorney will structure this.

---

## Decisions to walk in with

- Private foundation vs. public charity vs. donor-advised fund (drives both
  your deduction limits and your ongoing control)
- Donate the business interest all at once, or over several years
- Is the business activity "related" (exempt) or "unrelated" (needs a
  taxable subsidiary)?
- Your reasonable-comp figure and its market benchmark
- State nonprofit registration + charitable-solicitation registration

## Who you need
- **Nonprofit / tax attorney** — formation, the 1023 exemption application,
  the 4943(g) structure
- **CPA** — model the deduction (basis vs. FMV), UBIT, your personal tax
- **Qualified appraiser** — value the business for the donation

Ballpark to set up properly: a few thousand dollars in professional fees.
Cheap insurance against getting the asset-lock, valuation, or compensation
rules wrong.

---

## The bottom line
Your willingness to give up the assets and live on a small salary is what
makes this both legitimate and clean. The tax benefit is real but it is the
*consequence* of genuine charity, not a loophole — you get a large
charitable deduction in the gift year(s) (capped by a percentage of income,
with a 5-year carryforward), and going forward the business profits serve
the mission without ever becoming your taxable income, because you no longer
own them. That is exactly the outcome you described wanting.

*This is general information, not legal or tax advice. The structures here
are real and well-trodden, but the specifics — especially the basis-vs-FMV
deduction question and the UBIT structuring — must be confirmed by a
licensed nonprofit attorney and CPA for your exact situation and state.*
