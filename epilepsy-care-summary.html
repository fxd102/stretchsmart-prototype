Seizure Care — Information Summary

1 · Do these right now — safety

These do not depend on any decision about treatment. They reduce the risk of a serious injury while the rest gets sorted out.

Most important — non-negotiable No more baths — showers only.
A seizure in a bathtub carries a real, documented risk of drowning. This is the single most important change to make today, and it stays in place. During a shower, the bathroom door should be left unlocked, and someone in the household should know when the patient is in there.

If a seizure happens — first aid for the household

  • Ease the person onto their side and protect the head (something soft underneath).
  • Do not hold them down and do not put anything in the mouth.
  • Time it. Note when it starts.
  • Call 911 if: it lasts longer than 5 minutes, it is a first-time seizure, or it happens in water, near heights, or with any injury.

A few more safety items worth setting up

  • Driving. After a grand-mal (full-body) seizure, most states require a period seizure-free — commonly 3 to 6 months — before driving again. Check the specific rules for your state's DMV, and ask the neurologist.
  • Rescue medication. Ask the neurologist whether a fast-acting rescue medicine (for example nasal midazolam, brand name Nayzilam, or a rectal diazepam option) should be on hand for a long or clustered seizure.
  • Nighttime / alone. If the patient ever sleeps alone, ask about seizure-detection devices — for example a Sleep Activity Monitor (SAMi), the Empatica Embrace, or an Apple Watch seizure-detection app.

2 · The next step: a Level 4 epilepsy center

The bottom line The patient is on three anti-seizure medications and is still having seizures. When seizures continue despite two or more properly-tried medications, that is called drug-resistant epilepsy, and the recommended next step is a referral to a Level 4 (comprehensive) epilepsy center — now, rather than trying yet more medication changes first, and rather than going straight to a surgeon.

The most useful way to think about it: the family is not choosing between "surgery" and "more medication" right now. You are choosing the center where that decision gets made properly, using real testing. The center's evaluation is what tells you whether surgery, a device (neuromodulation), or a different medication plan is the right path.

The right way in is through the center's epilepsy (epileptology) department — not by picking a specific brain surgeon first. The workup finds the target; the surgeon, if one is even needed, is chosen afterward.

3 · Which centers

All are top-tier "Level 4" comprehensive epilepsy centers, chosen for a family able to travel. Which list applies depends on the patient's age.

Cleveland Clinic Epilepsy Center NYU Langone Mayo Clinic (Rochester, MN)

Cleveland Clinic has the deepest, highest-volume program; NYU Langone has a strong reputation in seizures related to head injury; Mayo is known for well-organized, thorough workups.

For a child / teen

Add these top pediatric epilepsy-surgery centers:

Boston Children's Hospital UCLA Mattel Children's Hospital

Cleveland Clinic and NYU Langone also have strong children's divisions.

How to choose between them

Wait times vary and matter — if seizures are frequent or getting worse, faster access is worth a lot. Cleveland's wait is often the longest (months); Mayo is often faster; NYU varies. It is worth calling all of them and going with the best combination of soon-enough appointment and reasonable travel.

Five questions to ask any center you call

  1. Are you a Level 4 (NAEC) comprehensive epilepsy center?
  2. How many epilepsy surgeries do you do per year? (Higher volume tends to mean better outcomes.)
  3. Do you offer the full range of options — resection, laser (LITT), and the implanted devices (RNS, DBS, VNS) — not just one?
  4. Do you do the advanced monitoring in-house (video EEG, and intracranial monitoring if needed)?
  5. How long from referral to the first workup appointment?

4 · What to gather and ask

The one thing that saves the most time Get a written, current medication list — a photo of the after-visit summary or discharge paperwork, or a pharmacy printout, is perfect. It replaces a lot of back-and-forth now, and every center will ask for it anyway.

Helpful questions for the current neurologist

  • For each seizure medication: the dose, how long it has been taken, and — where relevant — the blood level (for valproate/Depakote, whether it is in the therapeutic range).
  • Were earlier medications kept alongside the newer ones, or replaced?
  • Have there been recent lab checks and any side effects limiting the dose?
  • Since the last full seizure — has there been a complete workup (video EEG monitoring, an epilepsy-specific MRI)?

These help confirm that each medication has truly been given a fair trial — which is what makes "drug-resistant" the right description and the center referral the right move.

5 · Why this is the recommendation

The picture here is epilepsy that began after a traumatic brain injury and has continued despite several medications, including a full-body (grand-mal) seizure and ongoing focal seizures. In epilepsy care, once two or more suitable medications, properly dosed, have failed to control seizures, the evidence is clear that more medication tweaks are less likely to help, and a comprehensive center evaluation is the step that actually moves things forward.

That evaluation looks at whether the seizures come from one identifiable spot in the brain (which the location of the original injury can hint at), and whether that spot could be treated with surgery, a device, or a better medication strategy. Skipping ahead to a surgeon, or staying on the medication merry-go-round, both tend to produce worse outcomes than doing the center workup in the right order.
